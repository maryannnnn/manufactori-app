A catalogue of a few dozen products can live in a simple menu. A catalogue of several hundred, or several thousand, cannot. The website has to tell a buyer, a sales engineer, and a crawler which differences matter. This article is about that structure: taxonomy, page types, filters, links, and the order in which to build sections. The examples are hypothetical. They are not client projects and they do not report traffic or revenue.

Architecture is also a commercial decision. Every extra URL is a page someone must keep accurate. Manufacturing website design fails when the template multiplies faster than the product data. The Laser Made website project is a published example of a manufacturing site organised around product information. It is a record of that engagement, not a template to copy line for line.

@@ Start from the way the catalogue is already sold

Open the quoting system, the price list, or the engineering bill of materials before you open a keyword tool. Note the groups sales already uses: product type, size range, material, connection standard, industry, or the machine the part fits. Those groups are the raw taxonomy. The website should follow them unless a group exists only for internal production planning and means nothing to a buyer.

Write one sentence for each group that could become a section. “Ball valves, split by body material and pressure class” is a taxonomy decision. “Solutions” is not. If two groups overlap, pick the one buyers search by as the primary path, and use the other as a filter or a related link. A primary path is the breadcrumb. A secondary path is a way to reach the same product without a second copy of the page.

@@ Choose axes that buyers use to narrow a choice

The same part can be described by type, application, industry, material, and a technical characteristic such as pressure, voltage, or accuracy class. You do not need a URL for every axis. Use a dedicated section when people arrive with that word and when you can write something true that is not on the product page.

A workable pattern for a large mechanical catalogue:

  • Type is the primary tree: family, then range, then the product or the configurable range.
  • Material or a safety-relevant characteristic becomes a page only when it changes compatibility and when buyers search for it by name.
  • Application becomes a page when the job imposes constraints, such as washdown, outdoor exposure, or a named machine interface.
  • Industry becomes a page only when the constraint is different from the application. If food and beverage simply means washdown plus a documentation pack, say that on the application page.

Imagine a hypothetical fastening range. Buyers search by thread standard and by the equipment the fastener holds. The primary tree follows the standard. A second set of pages covers three equipment applications you genuinely supply. You do not create a URL for every combination of standard and equipment. The application page links to the standards that fit, and the standard page links to the applications.

@@ Give each page type one job

Category pages orient. They name the family, the choices inside it, and the reason to pick one branch rather than another. They should not pretend to be the datasheet.

Product or range pages specify. They carry identifiers, the options you sell, units, and exclusions. A configurable range with a shared datasheet is one page plus a selector. Fifty near-duplicate pages that swap a single dimension are a maintenance problem. Publish a separate URL when the identifier is searched on its own or when the specification change would mislead a buyer if it stayed in a row of a table.

Application pages explain the job. They state the conditions, the products that are normally suitable, and the products that are not. They link into the catalogue. They are not a second home for the full specification.

Educational pages teach a decision: how to read a pressure class, how a comparison between two materials should be made, what a drawing must include before a quote. They link to the products they mention and stay out of the product template. The article on semantic structure for technical pages is about how those pages are written. Architecture is about which URL owns which job.

@@ Stop thin pages and uncontrolled filter combinations

Thin pages are URLs that exist because a template can generate them, not because a buyer needs them. Signs are a repeated paragraph, an empty specification, or a title that only changes one word. They compete with the real category and they waste crawl attention.

Filters are the usual source. A visitor may combine material, size, and connection. That combination can be a useful on-page control without becoming an indexable URL. Let the filter update the list. Give the combination its own URL only when the set is a range you market, with copy and data you will maintain. Otherwise use a canonical tag back to the category, or keep the filter off the indexable URL space.

Duplicated content also appears when the same product is pasted into an industry page, an application page, and a blog post. Summarise and link. The specification stays on the product URL. The other pages add the context that belongs to them.

@@ Navigation, breadcrumbs, and crawl paths

The menu should expose the primary tree, not every application and article. Deep links belong in context: a category lists its children, a product points to the applications that use it, an application points back to the ranges. Breadcrumbs should follow the primary tree so a buyer can move up one level and see siblings. If a product is reached from an application, the breadcrumb can still show the product’s home in the catalogue. The application remains a related link, not a second parent that creates two competing URLs.

Crawl efficiency is the same idea from the robot’s side. Link the pages you want indexed from HTML that does not depend on a search box. Keep parameter URLs, print views, and internal search results out of the sitemap. The article on search visibility for complex industrial products discusses indexation rules in more detail. The architectural commitment is to publish a finite list of canonical URLs and to point internal links at that list.

For a hypothetical plant with 4,000 SKUs, the indexable set might be 80 family and range pages, 400 product or configurable-range pages, and 25 application pages. The other SKUs appear as rows, options, or documents on those URLs. The numbers are an illustration of the ratio, not a recommendation for your catalogue. Your ratio depends on how different the items really are.

@@ Connect the structure to search and to the way people buy

Early research needs the educational page and the application page, because the buyer is still naming the problem. Comparison and specification checks need the product URL. A request for a quote needs a form that knows which product page it came from, so sales receives the identifier and not only an email address. The structure is doing its job when those three steps are different URLs with links between them.

The same structure helps ordinary search and AI-assisted answers, for a modest reason. Both need a clear statement of what a page is about. A category that defines a family, a product page that holds the numbers, and an application page that states the fit are easier to summarise than one undifferentiated “products” dump. That does not mean an assistant will cite the pages. It means you have not forced it, or the buyer, to guess.

Internal search on your own site should use the same names as the public tree. If the site search calls a family by an internal code and the menu uses a marketing name, buyers and staff will both get lost. Pick one public name and use it in navigation, titles, and the quote form.

@@ Decide what to build first

Build in the order that removes confusion for the products you already sell:

  1. The primary tree for the families that generate quotes, with breadcrumbs and a written rule for what becomes a URL.
  2. Product or range pages for those families, including the specifications and the exclusions.
  3. Filters that help people narrow a list without creating indexable copies.
  4. Application pages for the jobs sales can document without inventing a case study.
  5. Industry pages and article sections only after the core tree is stable.

Pause a section if you cannot name its owner. A taxonomy without an owner decays into synonyms and empty templates. A smaller structure that matches the price list is more useful than a complete-looking menu that no one can update when a range is withdrawn.
